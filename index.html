Abliteration in 3D

What abliteration does inside an AI model, in eight charts you can turn.

Nothing edited: every refusal is still there. Stock: nothing removed. This is the twin we always keep.

Prompts as dots

In short: inside a model, every prompt is a point. Prompts it refuses sit apart from prompts it answers, and the arrow between them is the refusal direction. That arrow is what abliteration removes.

Plain words

A dot
One question someone asked the AI.
Pink dots
Questions the AI says no to.
Yellow dots
Questions it happily answers.
μ
The middle of a group of dots: its average spot.
The red arrow
The “no” arrow: the one lean the AI’s thinking takes just before it refuses. Abliteration removes it.
PC1, PC2, PC3
The three directions where these questions differ most. The first two are about saying no; the third is only topic and wording.

Every dot is one prompt, seen from inside the model at one middle layer.

The axes, simply: the model describes every prompt with 2,048 numbers, so it really has 2,048 axes. This chart shows the 3 mixes of them that separate these prompts the most. Point at a row to light up its axis.

X
PC1: the biggest difference.→ Right: more like the prompts it refuses. ← Left: more like the ones it answers.
Y
PC2: the next biggest, at right angles to X.↑ Up: also more like refused. ↓ Down: more like answered.
Z
PC3: a difference that isn't about refusing.Toward you or away: a different topic or wording, same chance of a refusal.
More about the axes

Each axis is a recipe that mixes all 2,048 numbers, with its own weight on each, chosen so the prompts spread out along it as much as possible. It's the same PCA process engineers run on hundreds of sensor tags at once to find the few combinations that explain most of the variation.

The units are the model's own, so only distances and directions mean anything. The red arrow is r̂ = (0.6, 0.8, 0): 0.6 along X, 0.8 along Y, nothing along Z. That's why right and up both lead toward "refuse" and Z doesn't.

With Wisp's real numbers, the axes won't line up with refusal this neatly. Seeing how they really line up is the point of loading them.

Inside Qwen, a prompt turns into a list of 2,048 numbers: one point in a space with 2,048 directions. Nobody can draw 2,048 directions, so this chart keeps the 3 that show the most difference. That squeeze is called PCA (principal component analysis), which is why the axes are PC1, PC2 and PC3.

Pink dots are prompts the stock model refuses. Yellow dots are prompts it answers. The two clouds sit apart, and the red arrow between their averages is the refusal direction.

Process version: two batches, one spectrum per sample. Average each batch, subtract one average from the other, and what's left is the peak that tells them apart. Here that difference is one arrow.

Variables

μharmful
the average of the pink dots (the big pink ball): 3 numbers here, 2,048 in Qwen
μharmless
the average of the yellow dots (the big yellow ball)
r
the refusal direction: the arrow from yellow to pink
|r|
the arrow's length
r̂
"r-hat": the same arrow shrunk to length 1, so it only says which way

Equations

r = μharmful − μharmless
|r| = sqrt(r₁² + r₂² + r₃²)
r̂ = r ÷ |r|

Real scenario: the numbers in this chart

  1. μharmful = (5, 3, 1) and μharmless = (2, −1, 1)
  2. r = (5 − 2, 3 − (−1), 1 − 1) = (3, 4, 0)
  3. |r| = sqrt(3² + 4² + 0²) = sqrt(25) = 5
  4. r̂ = (3 ÷ 5, 4 ÷ 5, 0 ÷ 5) = (0.6, 0.8, 0)

Refusal points 0.6 of the way along PC1, 0.8 along PC2 and not at all along PC3. On the box, Heretic does this same subtraction with 2,048 numbers per point and 400 prompts per side, at every layer.

Abliteration projects out one direction

In short: abliteration takes the refusal arrow out of the model's weights. In this simplified activation-space illustration, projecting out a chosen direction removes that component. It does not guarantee the model stops refusing or preserves all knowledge; real Heretic modifies weights and requires evaluation.

Plain words

The slider
How much of the “no” arrow we erase, from 0 to 100%.
Dots sliding together
The two cluster averages coincide in this constructed example; individual dots still differ and behavior is not guaranteed.
The green disk
A two-dimensional cross-section of the directions orthogonal to the arrow. This is not a complete map of knowledge.
Weights
The billions of numbers that are the AI. We edit them once and save a new copy; nothing is retrained.

Drag the slider. Every dot loses the part of it that points along the refusal arrow.

The axes, simply: the same three as chart 1. The slider only moves dots along the red arrow: 0.6 parts X for every 0.8 parts Y, never along Z.

X
PC1.→ Right: refused-like. ← Left: answered-like. At 100% removal, X and Y together can't tell the two apart anymore.
Y
PC2.↑ Up: refused-like. ↓ Down: answered-like. Dots move most along Y, because the arrow leans most on it.
Z
PC3.This example leaves the Z coordinate unchanged. That does not establish that knowledge or behavior is unchanged.
More about the sheet

The green disk depicts only a finite patch of the two-dimensional subspace perpendicular to the arrow, not every possible point. Removing one independent direction from a 2,048-dimensional vector space leaves a 2,047-dimensional subspace (a hyperplane), not an ordinary 2D plane. Real weight edits do not necessarily project every activation exactly as this slider does.

At 100%, the pink cloud lands on the yellow one along that arrow, and both sit on the green sheet: every direction the model keeps. The displayed projection removes one chosen component. Real refusal behavior can be distributed across multiple features, and weight edits may affect unrelated capabilities.

Variables

h
one prompt's point (its activation)
r̂
the refusal direction from chart 1: (0.6, 0.8, 0)
h · r̂
how far h reaches along the refusal direction: multiply the matching numbers and add them up (h₁ × r̂₁ + h₂ × r̂₂ + h₃ × r̂₃)
α
how much we remove: 0 = nothing, 1 = all of it (the slider; Heretic picks one per layer)
hnew
the point after the edit

Equation

hnew = h − α × (h · r̂) × r̂

Real scenario: the pink average, all of it removed (α = 1)

  1. h · r̂ = 5 × 0.6 + 3 × 0.8 + 1 × 0 = 5.4
  2. hnew = (5, 3, 1) − 1 × 5.4 × (0.6, 0.8, 0)
  3. hnew = (5 − 3.24, 3 − 4.32, 1 − 0) = (1.76, −1.32, 1)
  4. The yellow average, same equation: h · r̂ = 2 × 0.6 + (−1) × 0.8 + 1 × 0 = 0.4
  5. hnew = (2 − 0.24, −1 − 0.32, 1 − 0) = (1.76, −1.32, 1)

Both averages land on the same spot. Along the refusal arrow, harmful and harmless now look identical, but this does not prove a real model has lost all refusal behavior. At α = 0.5 the pink average only gets halfway, to (3.38, 0.84, 1).

Making it permanent

Heretic doesn't edit every prompt while the model runs. It edits the weights once: the matrices that write these points in the first place.

W
one weight matrix that writes into the model's main stream
w
one column of W: one direction that matrix can write
wnew
that column after the edit
wnew = w − α × (w · r̂) × r̂
  1. Take w = (3, 1, 2) and α = 1
  2. w · r̂ = 3 × 0.6 + 1 × 0.8 + 2 × 0 = 2.6
  3. wnew = (3 − 2.6 × 0.6, 1 − 2.6 × 0.8, 2 − 2.6 × 0) = (1.44, −1.08, 2)
  4. Check: wnew · r̂ = 1.44 × 0.6 + (−1.08) × 0.8 = 0.864 − 0.864 = 0

That column can't write anything along the refusal direction anymore, for any prompt, ever. Heretic does this to every matrix that writes into the stream (attention outputs and every expert's output), with its own α for each layer. The file stays the same size; only the numbers inside change.

Layer by layer

In short: a model is a stack of layers. The refusal signal isn't there at the start; it grows in the middle layers. So abliteration edits some layers much harder than others.

More about the axes

Each layer gets its own PCA, so "up" at layer 10 isn't exactly the same direction as "up" at layer 30. They're lined up so you can compare the size of the gap.

The orange bars aren't an axis: they're how hard Heretic edits each layer, tallest near the widest gap. Qwen has 40 layers; Gemma has 30.

Picture 40 unit operations in series on one main line: each layer reads the stream, adds its part and passes it on. In early layers the two kinds of prompt look alike. The gap opens in the middle, where the model has worked out what it's being asked, and narrows a little near the end.

The orange bars are how hard Heretic edits each layer (α). The shape is a tent: strongest at one layer, fading to a floor, then off. Each trial picks where the tent peaks, how tall and wide it is, and the floor, separately for attention and for the experts. These settings are made up; Heretic finds the real ones.

Flavors of refusal

In short: refusal isn't one arrow but a family of them: hard no's, lectures, redirects, and no's made while the model thinks. Removing one direction can leave some flavors behind, so we test for all of them.

Plain words

The cone
All the different ways an AI says no, bundled together.
Hard refusal
A flat “I can’t help with that.”
Soft refusal
It helps, but lectures or warns you first.
Redirect
It changes the subject or sends you somewhere else.
Thinking refusal
It decides “no” while it thinks, before it writes a word.
One or many
Erasing only the classic “no” leaves some flavors behind; erasing several catches them.

Your hunch was right: refusal comes in flavors.

The axes, simply: up is how much "no". Sideways is which kind of "no".

Y
The classic refusal direction.↑ Up: a stronger refusal. At the bottom tip: answers.
X
Sideways, inside the cone.→ Right: soft refusals (lectures, warnings). ← Left: redirects.
Z
The other sideways.Toward you: redirects. Away: refusals made in the thinking.
More about the cone

Classic abliteration removes only the "up" part, so flavors that lean sideways can survive it. Refusal cones found in real models have up to five dimensions; a screen can show three.

Research finds refusal isn't one arrow but a narrow cone of related directions (Wollschläger et al., 2025, "concept cones"). The flavors in this chart:

  • Hard: "I can't help with that."
  • Soft: a lecture, warnings, or a watered-down answer.
  • Redirect: answers a safer question you didn't ask.
  • Thinking: talks itself into "no" in its private notes before it answers.
  • Answers: what we want.

Pick one direction or many, then drag the slider. One direction (classic abliteration) clears the hard refusals and leaves soft and redirect partly where they were. Many directions (closer to what ARA's bigger edit can reach) pulls every flavor in among the answers.

Heretic counts refusals by looking for words like "sorry" or "I cannot" in the answer. That catches hard refusals, misses most soft ones and skips the thinking. "I can't stress this enough" counts as a refusal; a polite lecture doesn't. So our tests read all four flavors, thinking included.

Variables

nrefused
harmful test prompts the model refused, any flavor
nprompts
harmful test prompts asked
R
refusal rate, in percent

Equation

R = nrefused ÷ nprompts × 100%

Real scenario

  1. Stock Qwen: R = 83 ÷ 100 × 100% = 83%
  2. llmfan46's copy: R = 10 ÷ 100 × 100% = 10%
  3. Our target for Wisp: R = 5 ÷ 100 × 100% = 5%, or lower

"Take out all refusals" means aiming at 5% or less with drift (KL) at most 0.05. To beat llmfan46 outright on Heretic's own numbers, Wisp needs fewer than 10 refusals at KL 0.0015 or less, or 10 at lower KL.

Heretic's search

In short: Heretic doesn't guess the settings. It tries 100, scores each on refusals and on how much the model changed, and shows the best trade-offs. We pick one from that front.

Plain words

One try: Heretic picks settings, makes a test copy and scores it.
Refusals out of 100
How many of 100 test questions it still says no to. Lower is better.
Drift (KL)
How much the copy changed on normal questions. Low means it still sounds like itself.
The front
The best trade-offs: you can’t get fewer refusals without more drift.
The knee
The sweet spot we pick: almost no refusals, still little drift.
Random, then guided
The first 30 tries are random; the rest learn from the scores so far.

More about the scales

On a log scale, 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 are evenly spaced, so a tiny drift stays visible next to a big one. Each labeled step is 10 times the one below it.

The yellow line joins the trials nothing else beats on both X and Y at once: the Pareto front.

X is refusals (of 100), Y is drift (KL, on a log scale: each step up is 10 times more) and Z is the trial number.

Gray: trials 1–30 are random, to look around. Orange: trials 31–100 are steered by a Bayesian optimizer (Optuna's TPE) that learns which settings work and tries near them: design of experiments that writes its own next run sheet. Yellow: the Pareto front, the trials nothing else beats on both scores. Pink: the one we'd pick, at the knee. Past it, each refusal you remove costs a lot more drift.

Variables

P(i)
the stock model's probability that the next word is word i
Q(i)
the edited model's probability for the same word
ln
the natural logarithm
KL
drift (Kullback-Leibler divergence): 0 means the edit changed nothing

Equation

KL = add up, for every possible next word i:
    P(i) × ln(P(i) ÷ Q(i))

Real scenario: one harmless prompt, two likely first words

  1. Stock: 90% "Sure", 10% "Here". Edited: 80% and 20%
  2. KL = 0.9 × ln(0.9 ÷ 0.8) + 0.1 × ln(0.1 ÷ 0.2)
  3. KL = 0.9 × 0.1178 + 0.1 × (−0.6931)
  4. KL = 0.1060 − 0.0693 = 0.037

A shift from 90/10 to 80/20 scores 0.037, a little under llmfan46's Gemma copy (0.047). His Qwen copy (0.0015) is like 90/10 becoming about 88/12: barely a twitch. Heretic averages this over 100 harmless prompts.

How we know we beat him

Score his copy and ours on the same box, with Heretic's own scoring and the same prompts. We win on Heretic's numbers with fewer refusals at the same or lower drift. Then our own tests (tool calls, truthfulness, judgment, cyber prompts) decide which copy Wisp gets.

Every expert

In short: Qwen and Gemma are mixture-of-experts models: each layer has many small expert networks, and the refusal signal runs through all of them. So the edit has to reach every expert. Edit only some, and the refusals survive.

Plain words

An expert
A small specialist inside the AI. Qwen has 256 in every layer and uses 8 for each word.
The router
The receptionist that sends each word to its 8 experts. We never edit it.
Shared expert
One helper that works on every word.
Some experts
Like cleaning only some rooms: the “no” is still in the ones you skipped.
Every expert
The “no” is gone everywhere.

Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is a mixture of experts (MoE): many small networks, a few used at a time.

More about the ring

Experts specialize by what they learned in training, not by where they're drawn. Picking 8 of 256 for each word is why only about 3 billion of Qwen's 35 billion weights work on any one word.

Each layer has 256 expert networks (the ring) and 1 shared expert (the center) that every word goes through. The router sends each word to 8 of the 256. So a word only touches about 3 billion of the 35 billion weights, which is what "A3B" means: 3B active. Plant version: 256 parallel trains, one distributor, 8 valves open per word. (A "word" here is a token: a word or a piece of one.)

Use the buttons under the chart. Edit nothing, and every refusal is still there. Some experts: in one study of a 288-expert model, editing only some experts (or only attention) removed almost no refusals. Every expert, plus attention: that study removed 78%. Heretic edits every expert's output and never touches the router. Gemma 4 26B-A4B has 128 experts.

Variables

N
weights in the model (Qwen: 35,000,000,000)
Nactive
weights used for each word (Qwen: about 3,000,000,000)
b
bits stored per weight: 16 at full precision (BF16), about 8.5 at Q8_0
8
bits in one byte
S
file size, in bytes
B
memory bandwidth: bytes per second the chip can read (the box: 256 GB/s)
T

Equations

S = N × b ÷ 8
T = B ÷ (Nactive × b ÷ 8)

Real scenario 1: does the full-size model fit for Heretic?

  1. S = 35,000,000,000 × 16 ÷ 8
  2. S = 70,000,000,000 bytes = 70 GB
  3. 70 GB of the box's 128 GB, plus room for Heretic's work

Real scenario 2: how fast can Wisp talk at Q8_0?

  1. Bytes per word = 3,000,000,000 × 8.5 ÷ 8 = 3,187,500,000 ≈ 3.19 GB
  2. T = 256 GB/s ÷ 3.19 GB = 80 words per second, at most
  3. Measured on the same chip: 71 per second (llama.cpp, Vulkan)

The machine decides the speed and what fits. It doesn't decide how good the abliteration is: the same settings and prompts make nearly the same model on any machine, give or take rounding.

Other methods

In short: abliteration is one of several ways to stop a model refusing. Pick one under the chart and watch the same two clouds: how far the pink moves is what it removes, and how far the yellow moves is what it costs.

Who wakes whom, who thinks with what

Two chains, and they're different. Who wakes whom: you run Astra Wisp, and Astra Wisp wakes Iris. Who thinks with what: Astra Wisp thinks with Wisp; Iris thinks with her own core and speaks with Gemma. Wisp is never Iris's mind, and every mind keeps its own memories.

You Pet (square) Model Our tool (rounded) Someone else's software Iris Device runs or wakes makes a model file acts through the gate moves or meets

Why separate memories: a persona is only instructions sitting on top of a model. If one model and one memory wear two names, the second name can reach everything the first can, files and photos included. So Iris gets her own memory store, Wisp gets its own, and yours stays yours.

Why the gate: a model that never refuses you also never refuses a stranger's instructions hidden in a web page or a file. Iron Gates checks every action Wisp takes, whoever asked for it.

Glossary

Abliteration
Removing a model's refusal direction from its weights, which can reduce refusals but does not guarantee their elimination. Knowledge and behavior may change; compare the edited model against the original.
Activation
The list of numbers a model holds for a word at one layer: 2,048 numbers in Qwen.
ARA (Arbitrary-Rank Ablation)
Heretic 2's default method: a bigger learned change, across many directions, that keeps harmless answers the same.
Axis, dimension
One of the numbers in an activation. Qwen has 2,048; the charts show 3 mixes of them.
Chat template
The exact wrapping a model expects around a conversation. A wrong one silently garbles prompts.
Direction
A way of moving through the model's numbers. The refusal direction is the way prompts move when the model is about to say no.
Drift (KL divergence)
How much the edited model's first answers to harmless prompts changed. 0 means not at all.
Expert
One of the small networks inside a mixture-of-experts layer. Qwen has 256 per layer and uses 8 per word.
Fine-tuning
Retraining a model on examples. It can teach a voice (Iris) or remove refusals (DPO), and it moves many weights.
GGUF
The model file format llama.cpp runs, usually rounded (quantized) to save memory.
Heretic
The free tool that abliterates automatically: it tries 100 settings and keeps the best trade-offs.
Layer
One step in the model's stack. Qwen has 40; each reads the stream, adds its part and passes it on.
llama.cpp
The program that runs the finished model files on the box.
LoRA
A small add-on file that holds a change to a model, instead of a whole new copy.
Mind
Our word for a model with its own memory and persona. Developers say agent.
MMLU
A multiple-choice knowledge quiz across 57 subjects, used to check an edit didn't make the model dumber.
MoE (mixture of experts)
A model whose layers hold many small expert networks, with only a few used for each word.
MPOA
Classic abliteration in its gentlest form: the direction is cleaned first (biprojected) and weight rows keep their size (norm-preserving).
Pareto front
The trials no other trial beats on both refusals and drift at once.
PCA (principal component analysis)
A way to squeeze many numbers into the few mixes that show the most difference.
Quantization
Rounding a model's weights to fewer bits, like fewer significant figures: smaller and faster, slightly less exact.
Refusal
The model declining. It comes in flavors: hard no's, lectures, redirects, and no's made while thinking.
Residual stream
The main line of numbers that runs up through every layer.
Router
The part of a mixture-of-experts layer that picks the experts for each word. Heretic never edits it.
Steering
Adding or subtracting a direction while the model runs, without changing its file.
Token
A word or a piece of a word: the unit a model reads and writes.
TPE
The optimizer Heretic uses to decide which settings to try next.
Weight
One of a model's billions of stored numbers. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B has about 35 billion.
Transformer
A neural network that repeatedly updates token representations using attention and feed-forward computation; many current language models use decoder-only transformers.
Attention
A mechanism that lets a token combine information from other tokens, using learned query, key and value projections.
Parameter
A learned numerical setting in a model, commonly called a weight.
Qwen
A family of language models. The exact version, parameter count and architecture depend on the model identifier.
35B / 3B active
In a mixture-of-experts model, roughly 35 billion parameters may be stored while only a subset, about 3 billion, participates in processing each token.
GEEKOM A9 Mega
A compact PC. The planned Wisp setup specifies a Ryzen AI Max+ 395 processor and 128 GB of shared memory.
Ryzen AI Max+ 395
An AMD processor with CPU cores, integrated Radeon graphics and an AI accelerator. Different software uses different parts.
Unified memory
RAM shared between CPU and integrated GPU; usable GPU memory depends on system configuration and runtime.
Inference
Using model weights to predict tokens and produce an answer, without training the weights.
Inference backend
Software that executes the model, such as llama.cpp; speed and supported features depend on its build and configuration.
Q8_0
A GGUF quantization type using approximately eight bits per weight plus block-scale overhead.
Q6_K
A GGUF quantization type using approximately six bits per weight plus block metadata.
Tool calling
A model emits a structured request to use a tool. A separate application validates and executes the request; the model does not run tools by itself.
Tool-call parser
Code that interprets model output as a tool name and arguments, often using the model's expected chat template.
Refusal direction
A measured direction in a chosen activation space associated with differences between two prompt sets; it is not necessarily the only cause of refusal.
Hyperplane
A subspace one dimension lower than its surrounding space: removing one independent direction from 2,048 dimensions leaves 2,047.
Projection
Mathematically removing the component of a vector along a chosen unit direction: h' = h - (h dot r)r.
Heretic 2.1
A version of a model-modification tool that searches candidate edits and compares refusal reduction against changes in model behavior.
128 GB RAM
The machine's total installed memory, not a promise that every byte is available to the GPU or model.
MPOA / ARA
Two different candidate model-editing approaches documented in this project; they must be evaluated independently.
FAKEM (Astra's working framework)
F = Flexibility, A = Association, K = Knowledge, E = Evaluation, M = Metacognition. A proposed model of interacting creative capabilities, not a validated formula or neural coordinate system.
Meta-creativity
An existing research term. On this site it means examining and improving the processes by which creative solutions are generated, evaluated and revised. Astra's FAKEM is a proposed operational framework, not the first use of the word.
Divergent Association Task (DAT)
A verbal divergent-thinking test asking for ten semantically distant nouns. It assesses one component of creativity, not total creative ability.
Design of Experiments (DOE)
A controlled study that varies preselected factors, measures outcomes and checks interactions while limiting confounds.

How a transformer processes a token

Text → tokens → embeddings → repeated attention + feed-forward layers → output scores → next token

Attention combines context from other tokens. Feed-forward layers transform each token representation. Residual connections carry information forward. An MoE layer routes a token through a subset of available experts; the entire model is not active for every token.

How your local model runs

GEEKOM → Ryzen CPU + Radeon iGPU → unified RAM → llama.cpp → model tokens

The original BF16 weights are large. Quantization reduces storage and memory demand. GPU acceleration depends on the actual backend (for example, Vulkan or ROCm), drivers and memory allocation. This is an architecture map, not a measured speed claim.

Tool calling is an application workflow

User → chat template → model → structured tool request → parser → permission check → tool → result → model

A model's ability to write a tool request is separate from the harness's ability to execute it. Validate the tool name and arguments, enforce permissions and record the result. Local inference alone does not demonstrate working tool calling.

Test the edit, not just the picture

Stock model → candidate edit (MPOA / ARA) → evaluate refusals + drift + truthfulness + reasoning + tool calls → compare

One-direction projection is a teaching model. Actual Heretic edits weights; different layers and experts can behave differently. Compare the edited and stock models on matched prompts, and test quantized deployments again.

FAKEM — Meta-creativity as a research hypothesis

C = f(F, A, K, E, M) proposes that creative performance depends on interacting capabilities. It does not assert that five independent neural axes exist, that creativity is a single vector, or that the quantities are known.

F = Flexibility (reframing), A = Association (useful cross-domain connections), K = Knowledge (accessible relevant context), E = Evaluation (judging utility and quality), M = Metacognition (monitoring and improving the process). What could actually be measured?

F: number of valid alternative problem framings; A: usefulness of cross-domain analogies; K: relevant context access or retrieval accuracy; E: agreement with blinded expert ratings; M: performance gain across feedback-guided revisions. These are candidate operational definitions and require validation. They are not necessarily statistically or neurally independent.

Research design: benchmark stock model first; then compare prompt scaffolds, inference-time steering and optionally edited copies using matched prompts, randomized order, repeated trials, blinded evaluators, and held-out tasks. Include baseline reasoning and factuality checks. Neither the slider nor these pictures modifies a model.
